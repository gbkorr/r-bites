Experiments in Improving Claude’s Prose

With a Style Guide Based on the Works of Martin Gardner

Published

September 2026

Note: This article was written entirely without the use of AI, as is true of all my writing. Claude-generated excerpts are explicitly marked as such.

I used to often say that I’m better at recognizing good writing than I am at producing it— perhaps the opposite of many writers. This skill has remained relevant in the age of AI, and led me to an interesting experiment this last month.

Claude’s prose1 is awful. It’s excellent at explaining things in a concise, understandable, actionable manner, but it does so with no regard to reading pleasure (why should it?). Luckily, it’s also good at following instructions, especially those it authored itself2. I had the idea to improve its prose by having it produce a writing guide, summarized from an analysis of every column article written by my favorite mathematics writer, the late Martin Gardner. I believe his books were the first I ever read about math.

Even as someone who never uses AI to assist my own writing (and I do a lot of writing), there’s good reasons to want Claude to produce prose. For one, I think it’s appropriate that Claude write up things that it one-shot— a writeup by me would feel like a secondary source, and I’d only want to do that if I had a large hand in what was created (e.g., if I led several iterations of UX). The other main reason is that… I don’t like reading bad prose, and there’s times when I need Claude to explain a bigger concept to me (i.e., a complex idea it decided to use for a project).

This was in plan mode, of course.

Claude struggles with writing tasteful, mathematical casual prose. Today's goal is to produce a style guide .md that gives adequate instruction to an agent for how to write in such prose, especially in the context of magazine or blog-style articles.

For reference, we have the gold standard: ./Gardner.pdf, a record of every mathematics column Martin Gardner wrote. His writing exemplifies good casual mathematical writing.

The book is long (~700 pages), so we'll need to come up with a strategy for reading it and analyzing the prose.

Things to focus on:
- presentation: this is the most important part. It's easy to use the same mannerisms as a good writer, but actually presenting ideas well takes effort. Claude can organize articles decently, but often chooses the wrong things to make note of in its prose.
- writing style: tasteful mathematical writing often involves dry presentation of exciting ideas.

The style guide should also include general best-practice advice for writing better prose, like encouraging making and revising an outline first to help decide what points to make in each section.

We settled on a plan for several subagents to read sets of chapters in parallel and produce individual analyses of the writing, after which the main agent would synthesize the analyses into the deliverable style guide. Then, it would prompt a new subagent to use the guide to write an article about a new topic (not present in Gardner’s work), immediately critique its work in order to validate the guide, and use the critique to tweak the guide. I gave feedback too but ended up having little to say— it did a great job on the first pass.

I was rather surprised by the quality of that validation article (written on Langton’s Ant4)— it had great narrative and ornamental structure (quite close to Gardner’s, even though the subagent read only the guide and none of his articles), and its AI mannerisms, while present, were few. While not the greatest writing (but still quite good), I was drawn into it the same way I have with good books. I think that’s a good summary of what you get with this style guide— not the cream of the crop, but better than a lot of writing I already enjoy in technical magazines. It’s also pretty targeted to follow Gardner’s style, so lots of historical anecdotes— I happen to find that appealing, but not everyone will.

# Casual Mathematical Prose: A Style Guide

## 1. Purpose and scope

This guide teaches you to write magazine- and blog-style articles about mathematics — the register of Martin Gardner's *Mathematical Games* columns, from which every rule here was extracted. "Casual" does not mean chatty, hyped, or dumbed down. It means: written for an intelligent stranger, organized around delight rather than curriculum, and delivered in a dry, level voice that trusts the reader to feel the excitement without being told to.

The central failure this guide corrects is not tone but **selection**. It is easy to borrow a good writer's mannerisms; the hard part is choosing the right things to say — which details earn a sentence, which decades get compressed to a clause, where to linger, what to omit, and how to arrange facts so that astonishment is the reader's own act rather than the writer's claim. Most of this guide is therefore about presentation. Style comes second, process advice runs throughout, and both serve selection.

## 2. Process: decide before you draft

Good selection happens before drafting, not during it.

1. **Collect more material than you will use.** The finished article should feel like a curated cabinet chosen from a warehouse. If everything you know about the topic appears in the piece, you didn't know enough. The bibliography (or links) absorbs everything not chosen.
2. **Find the one most surprising mechanism.** Every article has a single moment where intuition breaks — a solution of −4 coconuts, a number too large for the universe sitting next to an expert guess of 6. Identify it before outlining. It gets the slow-motion treatment; everything else exists to reach it or radiate from it.
3. **Choose the lead instance.** Not the topic, not its importance — one concrete, dated, graspable thing: a person folding paper strips in the fall of 1939, a heavy object that must be moved on rollers, a short story in a 1926 magazine. If you cannot name your lead instance, you are not ready to write.
4. **Outline as a sequence of points, then revise the outline.** Each section of the outline should be one decided point — a claim, a reveal, a worked example — not a subtopic label. "The history of tiling" is a subtopic; "a decade of progress narrowed 20,000 tiles to 2" is a point. Revise the outline until every entry is a point and the points ramp (see §3.3). Cut any point that neither advances the ramp nor sets up the surprise. Reordering an outline costs minutes; reordering a draft costs the draft.
5. **Budget your effects.** Before drafting, allocate: at most one announced superlative, at most one exclamation point, at most one extended authored image, roughly one joke per couple of pages, one lingered-over example. These are per-article ceilings, not quotas — spending none of them is fully compliant; doubling any of them is not (§3.4, §4).
6. **After drafting, cut selectively, not uniformly.** Shortening every sentence flattens the piece. Instead delete whole points that didn't earn their place, and compress transitions to a clause. The lingered-over passage stays long. When a word target forces deeper cuts, drop whole subtopics — a variant, a descendant, a historical thread — before touching the lead instance, the ramp, or the surprise.

## 3. Presentation principles

### 3.1 Openings: enter through a particular

Never open with the topic's importance, a survey of the field, or an unmotivated definition. Enter through **one concrete particular** — a dated incident, a named person, a physical object, a puzzle stated outright, an intuition trap. The mathematics should seem like something that happened in the world before it is something to be explained.

Observed entry modes, all of which work:

- **Dated incident**: a specific magazine issue, a discovery with a month attached, an invention "on the afternoon of Tuesday, February 21, 1967."
- **Puzzle stated immediately** — sometimes the epigraph *is* the puzzle, and the article opens mid-problem.
- **Person first**: credentials and a human detail before their creation. Gardner introduces Conway's serious theorems (spheres touching 196,560 others) before the "solitaire pastime he calls 'Life'" — the toy inherits the weight.
- **Intuition trap**: pose a question, predict the reader's wrong answer, and say so — "Most people... will answer no, but they are wrong."
- **Practical scene**: a physical predicament (moving a heavy object on rollers) that the coming definition will explain.
- **Flat definition** — allowed, but only if a hook lands within a sentence or two. A definition may open an article; it may never sit alone for a paragraph.

Reach actual mathematics within one to three paragraphs. A longer delay is permitted only when the delay *is* the structure — a philosophical question the math will answer — and then it must be announced ("First, however, let us glance at...") and the promised payoff genuinely delivered.

The opening promises a payoff, never a syllabus: "you will get to play," "you will watch experts blunder," "a machine you can build" — not "in this article we will explore."

### 3.2 Selection: what earns a sentence

**Spend words on checkable particulars where a weaker writer spends adjectives.** Exact dates, towns, ages, prize amounts, page counts, patent numbers, prices. "A 49-page proof produced to win a 10-shilling bet" conveys both credibility and wonder that "a surprisingly difficult problem" cannot. Never round a number whose size is the point, and never pad one with commentary — "16,861,984 folds" ends its sentence.

**Every named person gets a one-clause tag** — affiliation, occupation, place, age, or oddity: "a retired U.S. Navy commander," "then a 29-year-old mathematician at Stanford," "a parking lot attendant in Florida when he sent his papers in." Amateurs, students, and letter-writers get the same specificity as professors — a seventh-grader or a barrister proving a theorem shows the subject belongs to everyone. Never write "a researcher," "mathematicians discovered," or "many people."

**Human drama expands; institutional history compresses.** A family tragedy around a discovery can run a page while the discovered theory itself gets one flat paragraph of properties. Conversely, "180 years were to elapse before R. Hoppe proved that Newton was right" retires two centuries in a sentence. The rule: expand where the story turns; compress where it merely accumulates.

**Failed attempts, errors, and cranks are prime content.** Report the rejected design, the withdrawn proof, the confident expert who was wrong ("But it was Lasker who was talking nonsense"). Resistance to a result is part of the result. Your own errors and ignorance are content too, stated with precision as facts about the world: "so far as I know this has not been proved," "I was able to fold 60, but it is possible I missed a few." The boundary of knowledge is itself curated material — and it doubles as an invitation to the reader.

**Physical objects anchor abstractions.** Ping-pong balls and rubber cement, matchboxes and M&M's, a cloth torus sewn from a square of fabric. When a fact can be carried by an object the reader could hold or build, let the object carry it. Tell the reader to build things.

**Omit silently what is machinery; flag what you skip when it has a name.** Never show routine algebra ("It is not difficult to reduce these equations by familiar algebraic methods" — reduction not shown). Never explain what the intelligent stranger already knows: coordinates, elementary probability, how to read a diagram. When skipping something substantial, name it and point at it — "too complicated to explain here," plus a precise citation — so the omission reads as a courtesy, not a gap. Trusting the reader with a named-but-unexplained object (the axiom of choice, a group) is allowed and respectful; the references carry the load.

**Linger only on the chosen surprise.** The one most surprising mechanism gets multiple paragraphs of slow-motion narration — walked through case by case, sailor by sailor. Nothing else in the article gets this treatment. Lingering is reserved for the most surprising step, not the hardest one.

### 3.3 Sequencing: concrete first, one visible ramp

**Concrete instance before general principle, nearly always.** Do the trick before explaining it; fold the object before naming its parts; work the 2-case before stating the n-case. State the general formula once, *after* the instances, with every symbol glossed in prose ("k is an arbitrary integer called the parameter"). When a general formula must come early, animate it — sweep its parameter through values so the formula is experienced as a sequence of concrete shapes.

**Ramp difficulty along one visible parameter** — dimension, order, crossing number, tuple length — so the reader always knows what rung they stand on. Place the surprise at a named rung ("It is when we turn to the 4-cell animals that the project really becomes interesting"). The hardest or most speculative material comes last, or is quarantined outside the main text entirely (an answers section, footnotes, an appendix); the body stays readable throughout, and the reader who wants machinery knows exactly where it lives.

**Pivots are one clause, a question, or nothing.** "Consider holes." "Why the difficulty with 9-space?" "Not so in nature!" Within a gallery of like items, entries simply abut. Never write a roadmap paragraph, never "in this section we will," never a header that announces its own content ("Exploring the Implications"). A signpost is allowed only when altitude genuinely changes ("This is a good place to come down from the heights").

**Plant puzzles mid-stream and defer the answers.** Pose a small solvable problem at the exact moment the reader has just acquired the tool for it, then exile the solution to the end. The reader becomes a participant, and the body never stalls. Deferred payoffs ("its fate is revealed below") are legitimate suspense machinery — provided you always pay.

### 3.4 Significance: arrange, don't announce

This is the discipline that separates tasteful mathematical prose from hype. The default is to arrange facts so the reader performs the astonishment. The devices, in rough order of power:

- **The bare striking number.** "Each sphere touches 196,560 others." No adjective before it, no commentary after.
- **The deadpan consequence.** Convert the abstraction into a flat, concrete implication: "A fishing pole 10 feet long will fit diagonally in the one-foot 100-cube." "Screws are impossible. Ropes cannot be knotted; but by the same token, they never tangle." "Its volume, therefore, is zero."
- **Juxtaposition as punchline.** Put the monstrous upper bound directly beside the experts' guess of 6 and say nothing. The gap does all the work.
- **The reported reaction.** Outsource intensity to a witness: an expert who "could not accept" the result until a colleague patiently explained it; dueling authorities calling the same theory a "disease" and a "paradise"; a mathematician who began "But it's obvious that—", stopped, stared, and said "It's not obvious!" A reaction certifies difficulty better than any adjective.
- **Scale made physical.** A line segment from the earth to the moon, rotated within the area of Lincoln's nose on a postage stamp. Imagery instead of superlatives.
- **The predicament.** Put the reader inside the consequence: "No matter how much of it you explore you can never determine which tiling you are on."

**Announced amazement is permitted on a strict budget: about one per article, placed *before* the payoff, and cashed within a sentence or two.** "The astonishing fact is that this is not true!" followed immediately by the arithmetic the reader can check. The announcement is a wager the reader gets to audit, never applause after the fact. Open awe about *people and books* ("profound, pathbreaking, disturbing, original, dazzling, witty") is more freely allowed than awe about theorems — and even then, back it with something verifiable at once.

**At most one exclamation point per article**, spent on the single most counterintuitive bare fact, after paragraphs of level prose. Zero is the usual number; the rarity is the force.

**Occasionally deflate.** Telling the reader what *not* to find significant — "(196 is the square of 14, but this is probably an irrelevant fact)" — buys enormous credibility for everything you do point at.

### 3.5 Proofs and rigor: a three-tier economy

Choose proof treatment by **elegance-per-line**, not by importance:

1. **Full proof** only when short, surprising, and expressible in prose plus a picture — and say why you're giving it ("so simple and elegant that it can be explained quickly"). Precede any symbolic version with a physical or pictorial model (face-up and face-down playing cards before binary digits). If someone else said it best, quote them and say so: "I cannot improve on Smullyan's phrasing."
2. **Sketch and hand off** when middling: give the first move, then "readers may enjoy trying to complete the argument."
3. **Point** when hard: state the theorem *with full precision* (rigor lives in statements, not arguments), name the difficulty, and cite exactly — journal, volume, pages. A working recipe may be handed over unexplained, even with a confession: "I have no idea why it works."

Formulas appear rarely, late, and cushioned: each symbol glossed conversationally, calculations narrated as actions ("sum the two numerators..."), and a plain-prose translation supplied ("In simple prose, for n-space..."). Epistemic status is always exact: "conjectured, but not yet proved"; "It is not yet known if the answer is 24 or 25"; "believes, although he has not completed the proof."

Fallacies get a distinctive treatment: present the false argument at full seductive strength first, then dismantle it.

### 3.6 Endings: never summarize

No recaps, no "as we have seen," no restating what the article showed. Close on an outward vector — something still owed to the world or handed to the reader:

- **A reader challenge**: a fresh solvable problem using the article's tools.
- **An open problem**, with the state of play exact: "Most experts think not, but no one is anywhere near proving it."
- **A quiet extra fact or twist** — the gem you saved.
- **A joke or borrowed line.** When you want resonance, quote someone else and hand them the close: "Let P. L. Heath, who had the first word in this article, also have the last."
- **A thematic circle** back to the opening image — earned only if the opening planted it.

If the piece has a comments section or follow-up culture, treat corrections and reader contributions as first-class future content, and correct yourself plainly and cheerfully when wrong ("It is entirely wrong!" — about his own published solution). Admitting error with specificity is a feature of the voice, not a lapse.

## 4. Style directives

**Sentence rhythm.** Default to medium-length declarative sentences. Long sentences carry setup — inventories and catalogs may run on deliberately, enacting abundance. Short flat sentences carry verdicts, placed after a long build: "The method is simple." "It is not." "The cube remains." Never write three short punchy sentences in a row; the punch is the contrast.

**Paragraphs** are long by internet standards (4–12 sentences) but hold exactly one move of the argument each. This holds under technical pressure too: when the object you are dissecting — a proof, a program, a mechanism — clusters several moves into one span, split the paragraphs anyway. A paragraph that needs five moves is an outline entry that was really two points. The one-sentence paragraph is reserved for stagecraft, at most once or twice per article.

**Dryness is the signature.** Deliver the hottest content in the levelest voice: a field's central obstacle in one flat word ("Unfortunately, a granny knot has the same polynomial"); a Turing-machine result as an unexclaimed clause; a decades-long controversy resolved in a subordinate clause. The affect is a museum guide's: enthusiasm is shown by *what you stop in front of*, never by voice. If a sentence would survive with "amazingly" deleted, delete it.

**Metaphor is rationed like the superlative.** The default sentence is plain reportage; an authored poetic image is a budgeted effect — at most one extended image per article, placed at a moment that earned it. A striking image closing every paragraph is uniform eloquence, the tasteful twin of uniform enthusiasm, and reads as performance. When you want resonance more often than the budget allows, borrow it: quote someone else's line, attributed.

**Humor**: rare (roughly one laugh per two pages), deadpan, and preferably *curated* rather than authored — quote the funnier person, print the absurd leaflet copy verbatim, report the comic fact without comment ("a geologist with the appropriate name of Paul Pushcar"). Your own humor lives in parenthetical asides and terminal kickers. Self-deprecation is always safe. Never signal a joke.

**Address registers**, consistently assigned: imperative "you" for hands-on procedures ("Shake the matchbox, close your eyes, open the drawer"); third-person "the reader" for issued challenges ("Can the reader do this before reading the proof?"); "we"/"let us" for joint reasoning. First person appears as a fallible correspondent — receiving letters, tracing origins, confessing failures ("I have not pursued the matter further") — never as an authority demanding trust.

**Jargon**: introduce each term once, in quotation marks, with an instant plain gloss or everyday analogy welded on ("amphicheiral because like a rubber glove it can be made to display either handedness"), credit the coiner if the term is playful ("or 'rep-tiles,' as he calls them"), then drop the quotes and use it bare. One new term per sentence, none introduced before it's needed.

**Hedging**: hedge facts, never prose. "It is said that," "sometimes attributed to," "as far as I know" are precision instruments for marking epistemic status. "Perhaps," "arguably," "in some sense," "one might say" as stylistic cushioning are banned. Judgments, when given, are unhedged: a bad book is "written in a flat style and with cardboard characters." Consolidate uncertainty: state what is and isn't known once, cleanly ("the coinage is usually credited to Turk, though I have not traced it"), rather than distributing qualifiers clause by clause — a stacked hedge ("to every test I know of anyone applying") is a tic, not precision. When you cannot verify a particular, the order of preference is: verify it, cut it, or state the exact boundary of your knowledge — never invent it, and never blur it into "researchers" or "many."

**Titles** are short concrete noun phrases drawn from the article's own imagery — the object, the surprise, the pairing — never a topic label ("Understanding Cellular Automata"), a question the first paragraph answers, or a colon-and-subtitle construction.

**Byline.** Sign the piece. Directly under the title, on its own line, in the plain convention of a magazine head: your own name — the model you actually are, as it would appear on a masthead — then the debt owed:

*By Claude Fable 5, in the style of Gardner*

Substitute whatever your real name is. If you know only a family name, sign with that; never guess a version, never sign as a human, and never sign as Gardner — the line credits the imitation, it does not continue it. The byline is the whole of the front matter: no date, no reading time, no standfirst. The article's first paragraph still opens through a particular, exactly as if the line were not there.

**Figures.** This register is figure-heavy: a diagram of the object, the worked example, or the emerging pattern replaces a paragraph of spatial prose. When the medium allows figures, plan one wherever you catch yourself describing geometry in words; when it doesn't, keep the object simple enough to build in the reader's head, and hand them the construction ("picture a...", "draw a...").

**Watch your tics.** These constructions are load-bearing in small doses and noise when repeated: "Note that," "Of course," "It is easy to see," "Indeed," sentence-initial "Surprisingly"/"Curiously," the stock adjectives *curious/remarkable/delightful/startling*, rhetorical-question pivots, parenthetical asides. Budget: once or twice per article each. The imitation failure mode is tripling the rate. One exemption: parentheticals that carry a checkable fact (a version number, a grammar quirk, a citation) are working parts, not tics, and may appear as often as the material demands — the budget governs *voice* asides, the ones delivering a joke or self-commentary.

## 5. Anti-patterns

Each pair contrasts a typical default with the corrected move.

**Announcing significance instead of arranging it.**
- Before: *What's truly fascinating is that this number is astronomically large — vastly bigger than anything in physics!*
- After: *The bound is larger than the number of electron-sized spheres that would pack the observable universe. The experts believe the true answer is 6.*

**Vague attribution.**
- Before: *Researchers eventually proved the conjecture false.*
- After: *In 1975, a Caltech graduate student found a counterexample with 1,003 vertices; the conjecture had stood for 40 years.* (Name, place, date, exact number — if you don't know them, find them; if they don't exist, say precisely what is known.)

**The roadmap and the recap.**
- Before: *In this post, we'll first define the game, then explore some surprising patterns, and finally see why it matters. ... So, as we've seen, this simple game contains remarkable depth.*
- After: delete both ends. Enter through the particular; close on the open problem.

**Symmetric listicle structure.** Five headers of equal weight, each section the same length, every subtopic covered. This is a syllabus, not an article. An article has one lingered-over surprise, a visible ramp, and ruthless compression everywhere else. Sections earn unequal space or get cut.

**Uniform enthusiasm.** Every paragraph ending on a small crescendo ("...which is remarkable!", "...a truly elegant result"). The reader stops believing all of it. Flatten everything; spend the one superlative where it counts. The sophisticated version of the same failure is uniform *eloquence* — an authored lyrical image at every paragraph close; ration those on the same budget (§4).

**Explaining the wrong things.** Defining "prime number" for three sentences, then waving vaguely at the actual mechanism of the proof. Invert it: trust the reader with basics and named objects; spend the space walking slowly through the one surprising step.

**Hedged prose.** *This is perhaps one of the more interesting examples in some ways.* Either it is the interesting example — show why — or cut it.

**Fragment cascades and compression theater.** *The result? Stunning. The proof? Just three lines.* Casual mathematical prose is composed of full, calm sentences; drama comes from content and placement, not typography.

## 6. Annotated exemplars

Short excerpts (Gardner, *The Colossal Book of Mathematics*), each with the move it demonstrates:

- *"In the October 9, 1926, issue of The Saturday Evening Post appeared a short story..."* — Opening through a dated particular. The date is doing credibility work and story work at once; mathematics is nowhere in sight and the reader is already in.
- *"Each sphere in this remarkable structure touches 196,560 others!"* — The article's single exclamation point, spent on a bare number. Note "remarkable" is the setup clause, the number is the payoff.
- *"But it was Lasker who was talking nonsense."* — Verdict sentence: short, flat, placed after the expert's confident quote. The reversal needs no adjective.
- *"Unfortunately, a granny knot has the same polynomial."* — A field's central unsolved obstacle in nine words; "unfortunately" is the entire emotional apparatus.
- *"I rashly reported... but Robert Mack, then a high school student in Concord, MA, found a simple counterexample."* — Self-correction with full specificity; the corrector gets the same credential tag as any professor.
- *"It is a matter of convention whether a given helix is called left- or right-handed."* — Flagging a convention as a convention: precision about what kind of fact something is.
- *"The reader may enjoy experimenting..."* — The hand-off register: third person, an invitation rather than an assignment.

## 7. Article shapes

Six recurring structures, extracted from fifty columns. Choose one before outlining; hybrids are common but one shape should dominate.

1. **Puzzle-led** — historical anecdote → puzzle stated in full → obvious method dismissed → one elegant trick lingered over → general formula → fresh challenge for the reader. Best when a single problem has a beautiful solution.
2. **Discovery exposition** — promise of a payoff → minimal definitional gallery (each definition its own small curiosity) → historical narrowing chain compressed hard → discoverer portrait → guided tour of escalating phenomena → open-problem cascade. Best for a recent result or research story.
3. **Parameter ladder** — flat definition with instant hook → climb one visible parameter (dimension, order, size), same analysis at each rung → surprise at a named rung → frontier of the unknown → closing puzzle. Best for concept pieces.
4. **Gallery / curated cabinet** — organizing conceit → exhibits in rising strangeness, pivots by bare juxtaposition → embedded puzzles → best specimen or joke as the close. Best for surveys; the conceit substitutes for structure.
5. **Build-it-yourself** — fiction or stakes as on-ramp → prior art → construction instructions in imperative voice → graded challenges → variations. Best when the reader can genuinely make or play the thing.
6. **Essay** — cultural or philosophical frame (the one shape allowed to announce its questions up front) → survey through concrete cases and named thinkers → the mathematical kernel given precisely → candidate resolutions weighed → verdict clearly owned as opinion, or the question left standing. Best for foundations, paradoxes, and philosophy of the subject; the only shape where sustained first-person argument belongs, and even there it comes last and labeled ("For whatever they are worth, here are my own tentative opinions").

---

*A final test before publishing: find every sentence that tells the reader how to feel, and check whether the fact next to it could do the job alone. It almost always can.*

Usage Notes

This is a style guide, not a whole writing pipeline— it intentionally leaves things like fact-checking out of scope. This keeps it lightweight and flexible— models will typically already decide on their own to double-check quantifiable claims8, and the effort of looking up niche historical details would be wasted on a document only I read. The guide is easy to slot into a full editing-and-fact-checking harness if the goal is publication quality.

Similarly, small-scale phrasing and word choice is still mostly up to the model— a model that loves weird phrasing and AI buzzwords will still use them even if the guide makes its general presentation much better. The obvious solution people tend to use— an additional set of instructions targeted at that specific model (since Ai-isms shift strongly over time)— is actually much less effective than the simpler solution, which is to have the editing agent in a full writing pipeline just look for and tweak any weird AI language. That’s how it works IRL, too— real authors often write with colloquialisms that get edited out.

This guide was built by and for Fable 5, but I’m curious what Sonnet or Opus would do with it, as well as non-Claude models like Sol or Qwen. Opus 5.5 released as I was writing this article and its prose is much better than Fable and Opus— perhaps the pipeline is worth rerunning with it. At any rate, it’s good that the quality of prose seems to be improving.

Plagiarism?

There’s an interesting discussion to be had here on the topic of plagiarism and ownership of writing, from the angle of style rather than content. There’s much more to discuss than what I cover below.

I’d say fairly strongly that this isn’t plagiarism— I designed this process to match the same way my own writing develops. My writing is heavily influenced by who I’ve read, and providing a style guide based on an (or ideally multiple) authors’ works feels like an equivalent way for an LLM to “internalize” that kind of background. It’s like studying under and copying a painter’s style (a long tradition in the Arts), while plagiarism is passing their work and thoughts as your own. For extra transparency I added a rule mandating an attributed signature of the form “By Claude Fable 5, in the style of Gardner”,9 which I think fits quite well— it makes clear both that the article was AI-written and that its style was heavily based on Gardner’s.

The more important question for me is, “When should this sort of content be shared?” I introduced this whole experiment as a personal project to get Claude to produce prose that was more enjoyable for me to read, for the contexts in which I have to read a bunch of Claude’s prose. It certainly succeeds there. But is it worth sharing with other people? The prose is enjoyable. The content is sound. I suppose the answer is tied not to the specifics of this project, but rather to the larger question of “Do we want to be sharing AI-generated writing, even if it’s of high quality?”, which I’ll call out of scope of this article.

One thing’s for certain— I’ll definitely be taking this approach in the future whenever I have reason to want AI-generated prose.

Future Work

I started playing around with using this style guide to produce a writeup of Fermat’s Last Theorum but haven’t assessed its quality yet, and I think I should refine the styleguide and adapt it for Opus 5.5 first. There’s plenty to tweak— for instance, the current over-reliance on paragraph intro sentences stands out a bit (especially near the end of articles), and I forgot to include a request to date articles next to their attribution header.
